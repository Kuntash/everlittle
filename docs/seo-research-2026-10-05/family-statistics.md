## 1. How far people live from family

1. **[C]** 55% of U.S. adults live within an hour's drive of at least some extended family: 28% near all or most, 27% near some, 24% near only a few, 20% near none. Pew Research Center, "More than half of Americans live within an hour of extended family," 2022 (survey Oct 18-24, 2021, n=9,676). https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/05/18/more-than-half-of-americans-live-within-an-hour-of-extended-family/
2. **[C]** Same Pew survey: 42% of adults with a postgraduate degree live within an hour of extended family, versus 63% of those with a high school education or less. Three-quarters say living near family is at least somewhat important (36% very important). Same URL.
3. **[C-inst]** The median distance Americans live from their mother is 18 miles, and only 20% live more than a couple of hours' drive from their parents. Quoctrung Bui and Claire Cain Miller, NYT Upshot, "The Typical American Lives Only 18 Miles From Mom," Dec 2015 (Health and Retirement Study analysis). Original: https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2015/12/24/upshot/24up-family.html. The NYT page could not be fetched; the figures were confirmed on the Boston Globe syndicated copy: https://www.bostonglobe.com/news/nation/2015/12/24/the-typical-american-lives-only-miles-from-mom/iSYQglkxaqA0VUe3WWHt7K/story.html
4. **[C]** Among U.S. adults with at least one living parent or adult child, 74.8% have their nearest one within 30 miles, and 35.5% have all of them that close. Choi, Schoeni, Wiemers, Hotz and Seltzer, "Spatial Distance Between Parents and Adult Children in the United States," _Journal of Marriage and Family_ 82(2), 2020 (2013 PSID data, adults 25+). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7537569/
5. **[C]** Same study: 13.2% live with a parent or adult child, and 6.8% have their nearest such relative more than 500 miles away. Confirmed in the authors' manuscript: https://public.econ.duke.edu/~vjh3/working_papers/SpatialDistanceFamily.pdf
6. **[C]** The U.S. mover rate was 8.7% in 2022 (about 28.2 million people), not statistically different from the 8.4% low of 2021; 26.5% of movers gave family-related reasons. U.S. Census Bureau, 2023 (CPS ASEC). https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2023/09/why-people-move.html
7. **[C]** Roughly 20% of Americans moved each year from the late 1940s through the 1960s; the 8.4% rate in 2020-21 was the lowest in about three-quarters of a century. Brookings (William Frey), 2023, from Census CPS. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/americans-local-migration-reached-a-historic-low-in-2022-but-long-distance-moves-picked-up/
8. **[C, dated]** About 59% of Americans live in the state where they were born (2014 ACS); Louisiana is highest at 77.7%, Nevada lowest at 25.8%. U.S. Census Bureau, "Homegrowns and Rolling Stones," 2015. https://www.census.gov/newsroom/blogs/random-samplings/2015/10/homegrowns-and-rolling-stones.html. I could not pull a newer figure (the Census API now needs a key); current data is in ACS table B05002.

## 2. Grandparents

9. **[C]** There are 70 million grandparents in the U.S., up 24% from 56 million in 2001. AARP, "2018 Grandparents Today National Survey," 2019 (n=2,654, Aug 20-Sep 4, 2018). https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/social-leisure/relationships/aarp-grandparenting-study/
   - AARP's newer study says "about 65 million grandparents" (see item 15). Pick one and date it.
10. **[C]** Over half of grandparents have at least one grandchild living more than 200 miles away. AARP 2018, same URL.
11. **[C, wording conflict]** The AARP research page says "about a third live more than 50 miles from their closest grandchild." The AARP press release says "29 percent live more than 50 miles away from their closest child, up from 19 percent in 2011." https://www.aarp.org/press/releases/2019-4-9-new-aarp-research-on-grandparents-busts-stereotypes-on-attitudes-employment-finances-and-lifestyle/. Quote one of them verbatim.
12. **[C]** The average age of becoming a first-time grandparent is 50; grandparents have four to five grandchildren on average, down from six to seven in 2011. AARP press release, 2019 (URL above).
13. **[C]** Grandparents spend $179 billion a year on grandchildren (about $2,562 each); 40% are employed, up from 24% in 2011; 11% have a grandchild living with them. AARP press release, 2019.
14. **[C]** 46% of grandparents reached grandchildren by phone in 2018, down from 70% in 2011, as texting and video chat grew. AARP 2018 research page.
15. **[C]** One in three U.S. adults 35+ (35%) and half of adults 50+ are grandparents, about 65 million; the mean number of grandchildren is 4.9. AARP, "Powering Families: The Essential Role of Grandparents in Care, Connection and Support," June 2026 (n=3,283, Nov 18-Dec 22, 2025). https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/social-leisure/relationships/the-essential-role-of-grandparents/
16. **[C]** Same study: 76% of grandparents say technology (texting, video calls, social media, photo apps, email, group chats) is a primary way they stay connected, and 50% of those 80+ text with grandkids.
17. **[C]** Same study: grandparents give the equivalent of 12.5 weeks of full-time work a year to grandchild care; 28% lived with a grandchild at some point in the past year and 11% do now; 9 in 10 gave financial support (average $2,654 a year).
18. **[C]** 8.0% of U.S. children under 18 lived in a grandparent's home (2017-2021 ACS); 60.1% of co-resident grandparents were 60+ in 2021, up from 46.8% in 2012. U.S. Census Bureau, "Grandparents and Their Coresident Grandchildren: 2021," 2024. https://census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2024/grandparents-coresident-grandchildren.html
19. **[S]** About 6.7 million adults 30+ (3.3%) lived with grandchildren in 2021, and 32.7% of them were responsible for their care. Search snippet of https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2024/03/grandparents-living-with-grandchildren.html; open the page before publishing.

## 3. Changing family structure

20. **[C]** Average U.S. household size was 2.50 in 2025, versus 3.33 in 1960 and 3.67 in 1940. U.S. Census Bureau, Historical Households Table HH-6 (CPS ASEC), released Dec 2025. https://www.census.gov/data/tables/time-series/demo/families/households.html (parsed from hh6.xls)
21. **[C]** 70.4% of U.S. children lived with two parents in 2025 (71.2% in 2024), versus 87.7% in 1960; 25.6% lived with one parent, versus 9.1% in 1960. Census Table CH-1, Dec 2025. https://www.census.gov/data/tables/time-series/demo/families/children.html. The percentages are my calculation from the table counts (51,202K of 72,734K).
22. **[C]** One-person households numbered 39.7 million in 2025, 29% of all households, up from 20% in 1975; married couples were 47% of households, down from 66%. Census press release, Dec 2, 2025. https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/families-and-living-arrangements.html
23. **[C]** Median age at first marriage was 30.8 for men and 28.4 for women in 2025, versus 23.5 and 21.1 in 1975; 58% of 18-24-year-olds and 16% of 25-34-year-olds lived in a parental home. Same release.
24. **[C]** 59.7 million U.S. residents (18%) lived in multigenerational households in 2021, versus 14.5 million (7%) in 1971. Pew Research Center, "The Demographics of Multigenerational Households," 2022. https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2022/03/24/the-demographics-of-multigenerational-households/
25. **[C]** Same Pew report: 26% of foreign-born Americans live in multigenerational homes, versus 17% of U.S.-born.
26. **[C]** 23% of U.S. children live with one parent and no other adults, more than three times the global share of 7% across 130 countries; 8% of U.S. children live with extended relatives, versus 38% globally. Pew Research Center, 2019. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/12/12/u-s-children-more-likely-than-children-in-other-countries-to-live-with-just-one-parent/
27. **[C]** Mean age of U.S. mothers at first birth was a record 27.5 in 2023; the total fertility rate was 1,621.0 births per 1,000 women, below the 2,100 replacement level. CDC/NCHS, "Births: Final Data for 2023," NVSR 74(1), 2025. https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nvsr/nvsr74/nvsr74-1.pdf
28. **[C]** There were 3,628,934 U.S. births in 2024; the general fertility rate fell 1% to 53.8 per 1,000 women aged 15-44. NCHS Data Brief 535, July 2025. https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db535.htm
29. **[C]** In the EU in 2024, the total fertility rate was 1.34 and the average age of women at first birth was 29.9 (Italy highest at 31.9). Eurostat, "Fertility statistics," data extracted Feb 2026. https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Fertility_statistics
30. **[C]** The global fertility rate is 2.25 live births per woman, down from 3.31 in 1990. UN DESA, World Population Prospects 2024. https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/content/Key-message-card3
31. **[C]** UK, 2024: 28.6 million households, average size 2.35; 8.4 million people living alone; 3.2 million lone-parent families; 3.6 million people aged 20-34 living with parents. ONS, "Families and households in the UK: 2024," July 2025. https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/birthsdeathsandmarriages/families/bulletins/familiesandhouseholds/2024

## 4. International and migration

32. **[C]** There were 304 million international migrants in 2024, nearly double the 154 million of 1990; that is 3.7% of the world population, up from 2.9%. UN DESA, "International Migrant Stock 2024: Key facts and figures," 2025. https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/sites/www.un.org.development.desa.pd/files/undesa_pd_2025_intlmigstock_2024_key_facts_and_figures_advance-unedited.pdf
33. **[C]** Same document: Europe hosted 94 million migrants, Northern America 61 million, Northern Africa and Western Asia 54 million; 48% of migrants were women or girls.
34. **[C]** Remittances to low- and middle-income countries were expected to reach $685 billion in 2024 (5.8% growth), more than FDI and ODA combined; top recipients were India ($129B), Mexico ($68B), China ($48B), Philippines ($40B) and Pakistan ($33B). World Bank blog (Ratha, Plaza, Kim), Dec 18, 2024. https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/peoplemove/in-2024--remittance-flows-to-low--and-middle-income-countries-ar. This was a projection, so say "estimated."
35. **[C]** Close to 20 million U.S. children, about one in four (26%), had an immigrant parent as of 2024. KFF analysis of ACS. https://www.kff.org/racial-equity-and-health-policy/children-of-immigrants-key-facts-on-health-coverage-and-care/

- **[S]** The Migration Policy Institute gives 17.9 million (26%) for 2023, 86% of them U.S.-born; its site returned 403. The 26% share is consistent, but the counts differ, so cite one source.

36. **[C]** 14.1 million people in the EU on 1 January 2025 were citizens of another EU country (about 3.1% of 450.6 million); 46.7 million (10.4%) were born outside the EU. Eurostat, "EU population diversity by citizenship and country of birth." https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=EU_population_diversity_by_citizenship_and_country_of_birth

## 5. Staying connected

37. **[C]** 81% of Americans talked with others by video call at some point since the pandemic began; 30% said video calls helped "a lot" to stay connected with family and friends (44% said this of texting, 38% of voice calls). Pew Research Center, "The Internet and the Pandemic," 2021 (n=4,623, Apr 12-18, 2021). https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2021/09/01/the-internet-and-the-pandemic/
38. **[C]** 91% of U.S. adults own a smartphone, including 78% of those 65+. Pew Research Center, Jan 2026 (survey Feb 5-Jun 18, 2025, n=5,022). https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/01/08/internet-use-smartphone-ownership-digital-divides-in-u-s/
39. **[C]** Smartphone ownership among adults 50+ rose from 55% in 2016 to 90% in 2025; texting is now their lead communication method. AARP, "2026 Tech Trends and Adults 50-Plus" (n=3,838, Sep 9-Oct 6, 2025). https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/2026-technology-trends-older-adults/
40. **[C, check base]** 73% of parents text with their young adult child (18-34) at least a few times a week, and 54% talk by phone that often. Pew Research Center, "Parents, Young Adult Children and the Transition to Adulthood," 2024 (Oct 24-Nov 5, 2023; 3,017 parents, 1,495 young adults). https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2024/01/25/parents-young-adult-children-and-the-transition-to-adulthood/. The percentages are confirmed, but check whether the base is parents or young adults before quoting.
41. **[C-inst]** Adults 50+ who saw family and friends in person at least three times a week had a 6.5% rate of depressive symptoms two years later, versus 11.5% for those meeting every few months or less; phone and email contact did not offset this. Teo et al., _Journal of the American Geriatrics Society_, 2015 (HRS, n>11,000). https://news.ohsu.edu/2015/10/04/research:-face-to-face-socializing-more-powerful-than-phone-calls-emails-in-guarding-against-depression-in-older-adults
42. **[C-inst]** 33% of U.S. adults aged 50-80 felt lonely some of the time or often in 2024 (34% in 2018); 29% felt isolated (27% in 2018, 56% in 2020). Malani et al., _JAMA_, 2024, National Poll on Healthy Aging. https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/12/241209122559.htm
43. **[C]** 1 in 6 people worldwide is affected by loneliness, which is linked to more than 871,000 deaths a year; social isolation affects up to 1 in 3 older adults. WHO Commission on Social Connection, June 30, 2025. https://who.int/news/item/30-06-2025-social-connection-linked-to-improved-heath-and-reduced-risk-of-early-death
44. **[S]** Average time Americans spent in person with friends fell from about 60 minutes a day in 2003 to 20 minutes in 2020. U.S. Surgeon General, "Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation," 2023. https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf. The PDF returned 403, so check it by hand; one search summary garbled this as "two minutes."

Rise Above Research, "2026 Worldwide Image Capture Forecast," Feb 2026. https://riseaboveresearch.com/rar-reports/2026-worldwide-image-capture-forecast-2025-2030/. This is a paywalled analyst model, not a count; present it as an industry estimate.
46. **[S, estimate]** Smartphones take about 94% of all photos, roughly 5.3 billion a day; earlier estimates were 1.81 trillion (2023) and 1.94 trillion (2024). Photutorial, citing Rise Above Research plus its own estimates. https://photutorial.com/photos-statistics/. Photutorial's 2025 projection of 2.1 trillion is higher than Rise Above's later 2026 figure, so don't mix the two.
47. **[C]** 82% of U.S. parents who use social media have posted photos, videos or information about their children (89% of mothers, 71% of fathers). Pew Research Center, "Parenting Children in the Age of Screens," 2020 (n=3,640, Mar 2-15, 2020). https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2020/07/PI_2020.07.28_kids-and-screens_FINAL.pdf
48. **[C]** Same report: 76% of sharing parents say easily sharing with family and friends is a major reason. Among parents who never share, 76% cite not wanting others to have access as a major reason and 71% cite not wanting sites to collect data on their children.
49. **[C]** Same report: 83% of sharing parents rarely or never worry their children will later be upset by the posts (16% sometimes or often), and 52% say other parents share too much.
50. **[C]** 68% of parents of children 0-4 worry about someone learning private information about their child, 67% about someone sharing their child's photos, and 52% that the child will be embarrassed later; 74% know a parent who overshared. C.S. Mott Children's Hospital National Poll, 2015 (n=569). https://mottpoll.org/reports-surveys/parents-social-media-likes-and-dislikes-sharenting
51. **[C]** In 2023, 57% of parents of children 0-4 used privacy settings to limit posts, 30% avoided posting photos or videos of their child, and 31% did not discuss their child on social media at all; 63% said other parents share information that could reveal a child's location. Mott Poll, Nov 2023 (n=614). https://mottpoll.org/sites/default/files/documents/112023_Sharenting.pdf
52. **[C]** 42% of teens in 25 countries say they have a problem with their parents posting about them (11% big, 14% medium, 17% small). Microsoft, "Civility, Safety and Interaction Online — 2019" (n=12,520, teens 13-17 and adults). https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2019/10/09/teens-say-parents-share-too-much-about-them-online-microsoft-study/
53. **[C]** Children aged 10-17 were twice as likely as parents to say adults should not post about children online without permission. Hiniker, Schoenebeck and Kientz, "Not at the Dinner Table," ACM CSCW 2016 (249 parent-child pairs). https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/FamilyTechnologyRules.pdf. This comes from coded open-ended answers, so use it directionally.
54. **[C-inst]** People's earliest memories date back on average to about age two and a half, a year earlier than previously thought. Peterson, "What is your earliest memory? It depends," _Memory_, 2021 (10 studies, 992 participants). https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/06/210614110824.htm
55. **[C-inst]** Children aged 5-7 recalled 63-72% of events they had described at age 3; by ages 8-9 they recalled about 35%. Bauer and Larkina, _Memory_, 2014 (n=83). https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/01/140124135705.htm

## 7. "Time you have with your kids/parents"

57. **[C]** Adults in households with a child under 6 spent 2.3 hours a day on primary childcare (women 2.8, men 1.7); where the youngest child was 6-17 it was 47 minutes. Adults with a child under 13 spent 5.1 hours a day on secondary childcare. BLS, American Time Use Survey, 2025 annual averages. https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm
58. **[C]** Americans spend about 4.3 hours a day with family (parents, siblings, relatives) at age 15, about 1.6 at 25, and roughly 0.9-1.0 from the mid-30s on; time with children peaks near 4.3 hours at age 39 and falls below 1 by 65; time alone climbs from 3.7 hours at 15 to about 8 at 80. Our World in Data, "Who Americans spend their time with, by age," from pooled ATUS 2010-2024. https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/time-spent-with-relationships-by-age-us and https://ourworldindata.org/time-with-others-lifetime (values read from the chart's CSV).

- **Caveats to state:** the data are cross-sectional, so age and cohort effects are mixed; "with" means in the same room, so calls don't count; overlapping company is double-counted; "children" includes grandchildren and any related child under 18; the series starts at 15.

**A defensible basis for the calculation**

- The viral percentages are not supported by this data. In the OWID/ATUS series, only about 18% of "family" hours between ages 15 and 80 fall by age 18, and 37% by age 25 (my calculation from the CSV).
- About 58% of lifetime hours "with children" from age 15 fall between a parent's ages 25 and 45 (also my calculation).
- The honest framing is that daily in-person family time drops by about three-quarters between 15 and the mid-30s and stays near one hour a day.
- For a calculator, use explicit user-entered assumptions (visits per year × days per visit × expected years remaining), as Tim Urban did, and label the output as an illustration.

## Popular claims to avoid

- **"75% of the time you'll spend with your kids is over by age 12" / "90% by 18."** No primary source exists. The most-cited page (1000 Hours Outside) gives no citation, and ATUS does not measure lifetime parent-child time.
- **"93% of your in-person parent time is used up by high school graduation."** This is Tim Urban's personal back-of-envelope figure in "The Tail End" (2015), built on his own assumptions of 10 days a year. Quote it only as his illustration. https://waitbutwhy.com/2015/12/the-tail-end.html
- **"46% of adults 50+ video chat regularly, up from 33% in 2016" and "78% rely on tech to stay connected" (attributed to AARP).** These appeared only in search summaries; I could not find them on the 2025 or 2026 AARP Tech Trends pages.
- **"Americans take 20 photos a day" / "the average phone holds 2,000 photos."** Photutorial's own estimates, with no primary data.
- **"A 2023 mover rate of 7.8%, the lowest ever."** Seen only on a real-estate blog (Point2Homes); the verified Census figures are 8.4% (2021) and 8.7% (2022).
- **Moorman and Stokes (2016, _The Gerontologist_) on grandparent-grandchild closeness and lower depressive symptoms.** The study is real, but I only saw search summaries. Read the abstract first and avoid causal wording.
- **UK grandparent figures ("63% provide childcare," "5 million," "£96bn saved").** These trace to charity and insurer surveys (Age UK, SunLife, Saga), not ONS. I found no official UK statistic on distance between grandparents and grandchildren.
- **Pew 2022 income split (84% vs 73%).** The fetch returned this for lower- versus upper-income adults, but it doesn't match the 55% headline base; it probably refers to "at least a few" relatives. Check the page before using it.
- **"$700 billion in remittances" and "X% of people have lost precious photos."** The first is a rounding of the $685B projection; the second has no primary source.
